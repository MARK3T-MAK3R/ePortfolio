MARK3T-MAK3R: Market Simulation Platform

Solo project, 2025 to present. Entrepreneurial

MARK3T-MAK3R is a real-time economic simulation I built on my own to see whether I could connect what I was learning in accounting, finance, investments, marketing, and strategic management into one working system. Users trade equities, bonds, and options through a platform with market and limit orders, margin, short selling, and a live limit order book, with technical indicators such as MACD and Ichimoku on the charts.

Underneath the trading platform is a simulated U.S. economy. Companies buy inputs, produce goods in factories, and sell to consumers directly or through retail stores, and those decisions feed back into market prices. The hardest part was keeping so many interacting systems consistent, such as how a factory's production limits flow through to a company's financials and stock price. I built it with a Python backend (FastAPI, Socket.IO, and Supabase) and host it on Vercel.

This project is the clearest example of my entrepreneurial approach to learning. I was not satisfied knowing how a limit order or a balance sheet works in a textbook, so I built a place where those ideas have to work together and where other people can use them.

  • Equities, derivatives, and bonds with multiple order types, margin, short selling, and a limit order book
  • Interconnected U.S. economy with supply chains, labor markets, and governance

Dashboard.py: Quantitative Equity Dashboard

Solo project. Entrepreneurial and inquisitive

Dashboard.py is a Python tool I built to answer a question I kept asking as I learned to invest: can a systematic process find good stocks and time when to buy them? It works in three layers. It screens for stocks with strong fundamentals using filters such as EPS growth, sales growth, ROE, and current ratio. It builds a portfolio from the candidates using Black-Litterman optimization with covariance shrinkage and hierarchical risk parity. And it uses technical signals, including Ichimoku, MACD, and ATR stops, to time entries and exits.

To test the process, I backtested more than 20 strategy variants with monthly rebalancing and transaction costs against a SPY buy-and-hold benchmark. Most variants earned higher annual returns than SPY (roughly 16% to 18% vs. 14.5%) but with a lower Sharpe ratio (about 0.75 vs. 0.94) and deeper drawdowns (about 38% vs. 32%). Along the way I fixed a Black-Litterman model that was producing extreme results and dropped a weekly rebalancing approach after concluding it was impractical and that survivorship bias was inflating its results.

The project draws on two of my qualities. Asking whether a systematic process could work reflects my inquisitive side, and building a tool to test the question, then changing it when the results did not hold up, reflects my entrepreneurial approach to learning. The main lesson from the results was to judge a strategy on risk-adjusted return and not on return alone.

  • Three layers: EDGAR/Finviz fundamental screen, Black-Litterman optimization, Ichimoku entry timing
  • Backtests compare strategy variants against SPY buy-and-hold

Honeywell Takeover Valuation

Group project, Apr. 2026. Growth-oriented

My five peers and I evaluated Honeywell as a takeover target through a peer analysis of profitability and operating metrics. We built 5-year pro forma financial statements and a DCF valuation from historical financials, management guidance, and analyst forecasts, which gave an intrinsic value of $217.26 per share. We then proposed two initiatives: a segment divestiture and an $11.7 billion share repurchase program projected to reduce diluted shares outstanding by 8.8%.

My specific task was the share repurchase proposal. I found that $1.7 billion remained under Honeywell's existing authorization, then proposed an additional $10 billion authorization, matching the size of the Board's April 2023 approval, for a total of $11.7 billion. I then updated the DCF in Excel to reflect the lower cash balance and share count. Paired with the segment divestiture, the program raised the implied share price to $253.21, 20.39% above the current price.

This project pushed me to work with financial statements at a much deeper level than I had in class. To update the DCF, I had to trace how spending cash on repurchases flows through the balance sheet, the share count, and the valuation. I came to understand that a buyback does not create value on its own, but by spreading the same equity value over fewer shares it can raise value per share for the remaining shareholders. That reflects the growth mindset I try to bring to coursework: taking a piece of a group project and learning it well enough to build the model and defend the result.

  • Peer analysis of profitability and operating metrics to evaluate Honeywell as a takeover target
  • 5-year pro forma statements and DCF valuation, with a segment divestiture and $11.7B share repurchase proposal

U.S. Bank Profitability Drivers: Tableau Dashboard

Group project with two peers, Dec. 2025. Inquisitive

Two peers and I analyzed financial and operating data from more than 4,700 FDIC-insured U.S. banks to find what drives profitability. Using correlation analysis, we tested how ROA and ROE relate to net interest margin, efficiency ratio, credit risk, loan quality, liquidity, and Tier 1 capital adequacy. We presented the results in interactive Tableau dashboards where viewers can filter banks by ROA to remove outliers or focus on a specific range.

The strongest relationships were intuitive. ROA was positively correlated with net interest margin and with noninterest income to average assets, and negatively correlated with the efficiency ratio and with net charge-offs to loans. ROE was positively correlated with equity capital to assets and with Tier 1 risk-based capital. Some results ran against intuition: higher noninterest expense, credit loss provisions, and noncurrent loans were each associated with higher ROA. That may reflect bank size or outliers rather than cause, and it is a reminder that correlation does not show causation.

This project reflects my inquisitive nature. I wanted to understand not just which banks were most profitable but why, so we tested each possible driver against ROA and ROE rather than assuming an answer. Building a dashboard that viewers can filter and explore pushed me to present analysis in a way that lets the audience ask its own questions.

  • Correlation analysis of 4,700+ FDIC-insured U.S. banks to find the drivers of ROA and ROE
  • Interactive dashboards covering NIM, efficiency ratio, credit risk, loan quality, liquidity, and Tier 1 capital
  • Findings: ROA rose with net interest margin and fell with the efficiency ratio and net charge-offs; ROE rose with equity capital and Tier 1 capital
